The engine, function by function

A walk through engine/ as of 25 July 2026 — seven files, about 2,200 lines, roughly forty functions. Read from the source, not from the docs.

They answer one question — should we move money out of this household’s checking account today, how much, and to which card? — and the interesting part is that most of the code is dedicated to answering no, correctly, for a reason you can name.

01The shape

The engine is a pure function of a Snapshot. No clock, no network, no randomness, no LLM. Everything it is allowed to look at is passed in; everything it decides comes back as a Decision. That constraint is what makes a sweep replayable years later, even after Plaid rewrites the underlying transaction history beneath us.

02models.py — types that refuse

The largest file (36 KB) exports almost no behavior. Its two real functions are tiny, and the rest of the work happens in __post_init__ validators on frozen dataclasses.

def money(value: str | int | Decimal) -> Decimal:
    if isinstance(value, float):
        raise TypeError(
            f"money() refuses floats (got {value!r})"
        )
    return Decimal(value).quantize(CENTS)
The boundary guard. Decimal(2.675) is not 2.675 — it is the binary approximation, which quantizes to 2.67, not 2.68. So a float does not get converted, it gets rejected. A wrong cent must fail loudly at the edge rather than round quietly in the middle.engine/models.py:42
def statement_day(year, month, day_of_month) -> date:
    last = calendar.monthrange(year, month)[1]
    return date(year, month, min(day_of_month, last))
The February clamp. A card that closes on the 31st closes on the 28th in February. It lives in models.py rather than interest.py — where it was born and which now imports it — because StatementCycle needs it too, and two copies would drift into a bug that appears in exactly one of them.engine/models.py:27

The validators are where the file earns its size, and they all lean the same way. Card.__post_init__ rejects a negative statement_balance, a negative minimum_payment, an APR outside 0–200%, and a next_close_date that precedes the closed statement’s own close. Every one of those refuses the direction that would shrink the reserve — because a smaller reserve buys a bigger sweep, and a bigger sweep is the overdraft the engine exists to prevent.

StatementCycle.__post_init__ is the sharpest example: it raises if grace_days < 21, the Regulation Z floor. A shorter grace would place a due date earlier than the law allows, pulling an obligation out of the 30-day horizon entirely. Bad upstream data must not be able to do that.

The property that changes the arithmetic

@property
def interest_bearing_balance(self) -> Decimal:
    if self.behavior is PaymentBehavior.TRANSACTOR:
        return self.statement_balance
    return self.total_owed
Behavior is not just copy. A transactor holds their grace period, so unbilled charges accrue nothing. A revolver has lost it, so their unbilled charges accrue from the day they posted. This one branch is why a transactor’s interest avoided comes out as exactly $0 rather than a small number.engine/models.py:468

03forecast.py — how low will cash go

Five functions, governed by one sentence in the module docstring: money arrives late and small; money leaves early and large. Every uncertain quantity resolves toward the end of its range that hurts the user. Not pessimism for its own sake — a forecast that is right on average overdrafts half its users half the time.

    if event.confidence < INCOME_CONFIDENCE_FLOOR:
        continue  # not money — a hope
    # Late and small.
    when = event.expected_date + timedelta(days=event.date_jitter_days)
    amount = min(event.amount, event.amount_low)
else:

    when = event.expected_date - timedelta(days=event.date_jitter_days)
    if when < snapshot.today:
        when = snapshot.today
    amount = min(event.amount, event.amount_high)
The asymmetry, in eight lines. Inflows below 80% confidence are dropped entirely; outflows are counted regardless of confidence, because an obligation you are unsure of is still one you might owe. Jitter pushes income later and bills earlier.

Note that min() is correct for both branches only because outflows are stored as negative numbers — one sign convention, everywhere.engine/forecast.py:108

Why _project_horizon() exists at all

It was extracted so balance_trajectory() (the chart) and conservative_low_balance() (the decision) walk the same path. Two walks would eventually let the chart show a different low than the one the refusal quotes — a bug that is invisible in tests and obvious to a customer.

Its first point is (today, current balance), taken before today’s own events, so today can appear twice. That is deliberate: a household whose balance only ever climbs is at its lowest now, and dropping the anchor would let the chart’s minimum disagree with the decision’s.

The spend model · a measured 5.5× error

_spend_per_day() carries a documented correction. The old model took daily_discretionary_high — a p90 of daily spend — and charged it against all 30 days, an effective reserve of 30 × p90_daily. But variance grows with √t, and that model grew it with t: a 30-day sum has 30× the mean and only about 5.5× the standard deviation.

Measured against three years of real daily spend for three household shapes, it reserved more than the household’s worst 30-day stretch had ever been. The over-reserve was $400–970 against a $750 default buffer — on a large share of days, the entire difference between sweeping and refusing.

The fix reads the household’s own worst plausible 30-day total straight off their own enumerated rolling windows (spend_30d_high) — non-parametric, their real skew and autocorrelation, explainable in one sentence. It falls back to the old model when None, and that fallback is what lets it ship inert and be switched on against a measured breach rate rather than an argument.

04decide.py — the gate ladder

This is the product. Read the refusal gates as features, not as validation: almost every hard thing that happens to a real household shows up here as a reason to do nothing.

decide() itself is about 70 lines, because each rung is its own named function. Here is the ladder in order, with the detail that matters most — whether a refusal at that rung is gradeable.

  1. _blocking_reasons() — funding account missing or not a checking account, connection unhealthy, balance older than two days, under 60 days of history, income coefficient of variation above 0.25, a blackout date, or a sweep still in flight.
    FUNDING_ACCOUNT_MISSING CONNECTION_UNHEALTHY BALANCE_STALE INSUFFICIENT_HISTORY INCOME_TOO_VARIABLE BLACKOUT SWEEP_IN_FLIGHT
    Refusal here is ungradeable — no projection was computed
  2. _coverage_reasons() — the portfolio is not attested complete, or a card exists whose payment behavior we have not observed for three cycles. Optimality within a portfolio you cannot see is not optimality.
    CARD_COVERAGE_INCOMPLETE CARD_BEHAVIOR_UNKNOWN
    Refusal here is ungradeable
  3. conservative_low_balance() — the forecast runs. Everything below this line carries a projection, and is therefore gradeable.
  4. _select_target() — highest effective APR wins, among the cards it is honest to sweep to at all.
    NO_DEBT NO_INTEREST_TO_AVOID APR_UNKNOWN
  5. _cadence_hold() — simply too soon since the last sweep.
    CADENCE_HOLD
  6. untouchable() → the surplus check — buffer floor plus reserved obligations subtracted from the projected low, refusing below MIN_SWEEP of $1.00.
    NO_SURPLUS BELOW_MIN_SWEEP
  7. apply_caps() — the per-sweep cap, the weekly cap, and the card’s own balance. These shape the amount rather than refusing outright.
    PER_SWEEP_CAP WEEKLY_CAP CLEARS_THE_CARD
  8. claimable_interest_avoided() — attach the savings claim, or attach nothing at all.
    INTEREST_AVOIDED

Why the cadence hold sits at rung 05, not rung 01

This placement is the whole design, and it is the best single example of how the file thinks. A blocking reason short-circuits before the forecast runs, so its refusal carries no projected_low_balance — and grade() refuses to grade a decision without one, on the grounds that inventing a projection would corrupt the calibration distribution with zeros that look like perfect forecasts.

Gate the cadence up at rung 01 and six days in seven become ungradeable. The engine would still observe daily and still be wrong daily; it would simply no longer be measured daily. The calibration distribution — the one asset the strategy says compounds — would lose most of its data to a rule that has nothing to do with forecasting.

The rule

The cadence limits what we do, never what we know. Daily data, weekly money.

obligation_in_horizon() — the two-term reserve

The subtlest function in the engine, and one where the naive version opens a hole rather than merely under-reserving.

def obligation_in_horizon(card: Card, horizon_end: date) -> Decimal:
    total = ZERO

    # 1. The statement that has already closed. Legally due.
    if card.statement_due_date <= horizon_end:
        total += _behavior_amount(card, card.statement_balance)

    # 2. The statement that has NOT closed yet, but will close
    #    *and* come due inside the horizon.
    next_due = card.cycle.due_for(card.next_close_date)
    if next_due <= horizon_end:
        total += _behavior_amount(card, card.unbilled_balance)

    return total
Term 1 alone leaves a gap. The moment the closed statement is paid, the next one has not closed yet — so the card looks like it owes nothing and the reserve falls to zero for the last third of every cycle while charges pile onto it.

And the forecast cannot cover it either, because forecast.py skips CARD_PAYMENT events unconditionally, on the event tag alone. In that window neither side accounts for the obligation. A double-miss, in the unsafe direction.

Term 2 activates once the next close is within horizon − grace days — precisely the window term 1 leaves empty. The two terms tile the cycle with no gap, and that is what makes the forecast’s skip safe.engine/decide.py:266

_behavior_amount() — the minimum is not what they pay

Three behaviors, three different reserves against the same statement:

BehaviorReservedWhy
TRANSACTOR the whole statement They clear it. Reserving a $40 minimum against a $2,000 statement under-reserves by $1,960 and overdraws them on the 20th.
MINIMUM_ONLY min(minimum, statement) What they actually pay, capped at what is owed.
REVOLVER observed_monthly_payment, floored at the minimum, capped at the statement Their habitual payment — which is also the counterfactual the interest claim is measured against.

The docstring names the bug precisely: the minimum is what the issuer will accept; this is what the household pays. Reserving the first while the second leaves checking is the entire failure.

The three functions exported for the grader

untouchable(), apply_caps() and would_sweep() are public for exactly one reason: outcome.py imports them.

The grader has to answer “how much was actually safe, in hindsight?” and “what would we have swept with a perfect forecast?” — and it must use the identical definitions. Two copies of the cap ladder would drift, and the day they drifted, the calibration numbers would quietly start measuring an engine that never shipped.

would_sweep() is the counterfactual itself: feed it the realized low and it answers what decide() would have moved knowing what we now know, obeying every guardrail the user set. It also opens by re-running _coverage_reasons() and returning zero — otherwise hindsight would price a refusal we were right to make as a cost, and the grader would slowly learn to talk the engine out of it.

_select_target() — the transactor exclusion

Highest APR wins, but a transactor is never a target. They pay no interest — the grace period already does exactly what our sweep claims to do. Moving their cash onto a card they were going to clear anyway is a prepayment, not a saving, and taking a share of it would be charging for nothing. They are still fully reserved against by untouchable(); they are simply not somewhere we can honestly send money.

The ranking also degrades in steps rather than failing outright: a single targetable card needs no APR at all, since there is nothing to rank. Several cards with no APR between them returns APR_UNKNOWN — because paying the wrong card looks exactly like working while quietly destroying the entire point.

05interest.py — what a sweep saves

Three public functions computing one number: the figure the user is shown (“that’s $31 of interest you won’t pay”) and the figure the company is graded on. They must never disagree, so there is exactly one place that computes it.

The model accrues daily at apr / 365 with no intra-cycle compounding, posts accrued interest at each statement close, and lands payments at the start of their day. Daily accrual is not incidental: this product sweeps every day, so when in the cycle a dollar lands is exactly the thing we are being paid to get right. A monthly-periodic-rate model would value a day-2 sweep and a day-29 sweep identically — and be wrong in the direction of over-claiming, on a figure we put in front of the customer.

Four ways it declines to answer

No APRNone Plaid does not report it for many issuers. A figure that looks computed but was invented is worse than no figure.

The payments never amortizeraises There is no payoff, so there is no total interest, and no honest answer exists. That household is drowning; the model says so rather than returning a plausible number.

The two layers, and why both exist

def claimable_interest_avoided(card, sweep, on) -> Decimal | None:
    if card.observed_monthly_payment is None:
        return None

    try:
        return interest_avoided(
            card, sweep, on, card.observed_monthly_payment
        )
    except ValueError:
        return None
Same computation, two contracts. interest_avoided() is the model: asked about a debt with no payoff, it raises, because a caller that wanted an answer has a bug.

claimable_interest_avoided() is the product: decide() must be total. A card with bad data is not a reason to crash somebody’s daily decision — the sweep is still correct and still safe, we simply cannot say what it saved.

None here means exactly one thing: no claim can honestly be made. The caller then emits no INTEREST_AVOIDED reason, so there is no code path anywhere that can render an invented number.engine/interest.py:243

The counterfactual is an argument, not a default

Notice what total_interest() takes: monthly_payment, passed in explicitly. Card.minimum_payment is not an input to this module at all, and a test asserts that changing it does not move the claim by a cent.

The reason: users of this product already pay more than the minimum — that is why they have idle cash. Crediting our sweep with interest they were never going to pay anyway inflates the one number the company is graded on. Measuring against their own trajectory is the harder, smaller, honest number.

The same instinct produced observed_monthly_charges. Without it, the model projected a balance that could only ever shrink, so a household charging $1,500 a month to the card we are sweeping against got a payoff date that never arrives and an interest-avoided figure overstated by construction. Charges post at the close, before the payment — which understates the days they spend accruing, so it understates the interest, so it understates what we claim to have saved. Wrong in the safe direction, deliberately.

06outcome.py — settling the bet

decide() emits a prediction. Until this module existed, nothing in the repo ever settled it — the engine’s central claim (“this $220 is not needed”) had never once been checked against the future that followed it. grade() is what makes the engine falsifiable, and therefore what makes every later improvement to it defensible rather than merely plausible.

The signature is the safety property

grade() does not take “the realized balances.” It takes the household’s untouched cash movement and applies the decision’s sweep itself.

A replay that grades each day against untouched history never compounds the effect of its own sweeps, and will systematically understate breach risk — reporting a tail that would only have been real if we had never acted. That is the difference between a shadow-mode report and a shadow-mode lie, and it is not the sort of thing to leave to a caller’s diligence. So the caller cannot forget to apply the sweep, because the caller is not the one who applies it.engine/outcome.py:147

settles_after_days defaults to 0 — the sweep is charged against the funding account on the day we decided, not when ACH would plausibly have posted it. That is the assumption most likely to find a breach rather than excuse one. A grader that flatters itself is worse than no grader, because it converts an unknown risk into a false sense of a known one.

The three fields worth understanding

  • projection_error — signed, and measured against the household’s unswept path. projected_low_balance is a projection of the pre-sweep trajectory; the sweep is decided from it, not included in it. Comparing against the post-sweep low would fold the entire size of our own sweep into the “error”, making the engine look wildly optimistic precisely on the days it swept hardest and was right. The sign convention is load-bearing forever — a flip silently inverts the dial every future calibration decision is read off.
  • sweep_caused_overdraft — low < ZERO <= unswept. Not “they went below zero”, but “they went below zero and would not have without us”. A household that overdrafts on its own is not our doing, and folding those in would make the guardrail metric meaningless: it would be dominated by lives we never touched.
  • false_refusal_cost — should_have_swept − swept, floored at zero. It fires on an under-sweep, not only on an outright refusal — which matters enormously, because the spend-model error in §03 does not refuse, it under-sweeps. A metric that counted only refusals would have been blind to the largest error in the engine.

_low_balance() is the private walk underneath all of it, and its opening is the balance at the end of today, with the ledger covering the days after. Applying day zero again would double-count it — and a grader that double-counts is a grader that invents breaches.

07explain.py — codes to English

Two public functions and one large match statement. render() maps a single Reason to a sentence; explain() maps a whole Decision to a tuple of them. That is the entire module, and its value is in what it makes impossible.

  • Copy edits cannot break the decision. Changing “paused” to “snoozed” is a change to this module, not to a financial calculation.
  • The audit log stays stable. A decision persisted in 2026 still means the same thing in 2029 after three rounds of UI copy. The code is the record; the sentence is a view of it.
  • The LLM has something to narrate from. Handed a code and its parameters, a model can write a sentence for this particular household. Handed a finished sentence, all it can do is paraphrase — and any paraphrase of a financial claim is a chance to change its meaning. The LLM is never in the decision path.
  • Translation becomes possible at all, rather than a rewrite of decide().

The tone is deliberate too: a refusal must never read like an error. “You have no debt left to pay. Nothing to do — congratulations.” The user is not being denied anything; they are being told their money is staying put, and why.

There is a small, revealing detail in the CADENCE_HOLD branch. The sentence is built in two pieces because last_sweep_amount may be None — and when it is, the copy says only when we paid, never a made-up figure. The same rule as everywhere else, applied to a sentence: the absence is structural, not a formatting accident.

08The four rules that explain the rest

Once these are visible, most individual functions stop needing an explanation.

  1. Bad data must never buy a bigger sweep. Every validator, clamp and default resolves toward the reserve being larger. Where a default has to be permissive (days_since_last_sweep = None), the docstring argues explicitly why that one cannot make any single sweep larger.
  2. No claim we cannot stand behind. Unknown APR, estimated APR, unobserved payment history, a debt that does not amortize — each produces None and no Reason, never a plausible number. The absence of a claim is a designed output.
  3. One implementation, or two will drift. statement_day() moved to models.py. _project_horizon() was extracted so the chart and the decision share a walk. untouchable() and apply_caps() are exported so the grader measures the engine that shipped. The stated failure mode is always the same: the day they drift, one of them is silently wrong and nothing says so.
  4. Codes, not sentences. The decision emits structured facts. Prose lives in a separate module, and the LLM narrates from the facts rather than passing strings through.

09Where to start reading

If you open one file, open engine/decide.py and read decide() at line 399 top to bottom — it is about 70 lines and every helper it calls is named for what it decides. Then jump to obligation_in_horizon() at line 266, which is the single densest piece of reasoning in the engine, and _cadence_hold() at line 114 for the clearest statement of why placement in the ladder is itself a design decision.
